Abstract

This study examines the impact of supply chain disruptions on integrated aerospace companies, with a focus on how global interdependence increases systemic vulnerability in high-precision aerospace manufacturing. Although vertically integrated firms such as SpaceX, Boeing, Blue Origin, and emerging aerospace players attempt to internalise production, they remain dependent on international suppliers for critical components including semiconductors, advanced alloys, and avionics systems. This dependence exposes the sector to disruptions arising from geopolitical tensions, pandemics, labour instability, and logistical constraints.

A mixed-method approach was employed, combining case study analysis (notably the Boeing 787 Dreamliner programme), secondary industry reports, and primary survey-based insights supplemented by expert perspectives. Findings indicate that disruptions consistently lead to production delays, increased operational costs, and reduced system-wide efficiency. Survey responses further suggest a limited public understanding of supply chain risk, with most perceptions confined to delivery delays rather than deeper structural vulnerabilities.

The study concludes that while digital supply chain integration and supplier diversification enhance short-term resilience, long-term stability requires strategic investment in domestic capability development and multi-tiered resilient supply chain structures.

Pages 3–4

Methodology

The Boeing 787 Dreamliner programme is the main case study, with comparisons drawn from ISRO and Skyroot Aerospace in India. The author combines industry reports and academic literature with an online survey of 25 respondents and structured interviews with two professionals in manufacturing and supply chain management.

Pages 4–9

Findings

Late or out-of-specification components held up final assembly of the 787, increasing costs and forcing engineers to rework completed aircraft. Most survey respondents recognised delivery delays as a sign of disruption; fewer identified the underlying risk of relying on a small number of suppliers.

The paper recommends diversifying suppliers and improving digital coordination in the near term, while investing in domestic production capacity over the longer term. Better information can help manufacturers respond sooner, but it cannot replace a missing part.
